The website was never the problem

Checked live, and confirmed three times over in the data

The obvious suspicion on a morning like that is that the quiz broke. It did not, and the store's own event data proves it — because it records people starting the quiz separately from finishing it.

Did the people who started it finish?

Steady across all three phases. Anyone who actually engaged with the quiz completed it just as reliably throughout.

What was checked

The live site was opened and tested directly: the homepage and the quiz page both load without errors, the start button works, the quiz advances through its questions, and the tracking fires correctly at each step. Combined with a completion rate that never moved, the conclusion is unambiguous — the traffic bought on that morning simply had no interest in a cricket bat quiz. That covers the quiz itself. What happens after the quiz, once someone puts a bat in the cart, is a separate matter — and it is where the money currently stops.

Where the money stops

Store events, all traffic — not only visitors from ads

The campaign is doing its job: it produces leads, and a healthy number of those leads put a bat in the cart. Almost none of them reach a checkout. This is the single biggest thing standing between the campaign and revenue, and it sits on the store rather than in the ad account.

On day one, 72 adds to cart produced not a single checkout. For comparison, in the week before the campaign the store recorded 24 checkouts started and two completed purchases on far less traffic — so the checkout works, and the purchase tracking works. Something is stopping this traffic specifically.

What a customer actually hits

Adding a bat to the cart and pressing Checkout was tested directly. The cart works. Pressing Checkout then opens a Razorpay Magic Checkout window whose first and only step is "enter mobile number to continue", followed by a one-time password. Before that is done there is no shipping information, no order total with delivery, no payment options and no guest option — just a request for a phone number.

The bat in that test was ₹27,998. Asking someone to hand over a phone number and verify it by OTP before they can even see what delivery costs is a heavy ask on a purchase of that size, and it lines up exactly with 115 adds to cart producing three checkouts.

There is a second, smaller consequence. Because the checkout events fire inside that window, the store only records a checkout after the phone step is completed — so the drop-off is largely invisible in reporting, and Meta cannot learn from it either.

Worth keeping in proportion

A ₹27,998 English willow bat is not an impulse purchase. Even with a frictionless checkout, very few people buy one within hours of taking a quiz — the decision takes days, and usually a conversation. The campaign is optimised for leads, and it is delivering them at nine to ten rupees each. The 146 leads are the asset here; they arrived with a name, an email and a phone number. The checkout should still be fixed, but purchases in the first forty-eight hours were never the measure of whether this is working.

Reading the numbers. "Leads" are the campaign's optimisation event, offsite_conversion.fb_pixel_lead, on Meta's default attribution window. 9 September combines the original campaign (paused at 3:54 PM) and the rebuilt one that replaced it, since both ran that day. Store-side event counts run higher than Meta's attributed leads because they include visitors who did not arrive from an ad. Cost per lead is spend ÷ leads within each row, so rows with no leads show no cost per lead rather than a zero.

Caveats. Two days, still inside the learning phase, and the rebuilt campaign is only a few hours old. Every split below the campaign level is a small sample; the Stories, 55+ and women's rows in particular are too thin to act on alone.
